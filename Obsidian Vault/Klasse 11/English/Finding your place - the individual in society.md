# Persuasion Techniques

| Technique                  | What it means                                                                          | Typical effect on the reader                                                                  | Example                                                              |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Appeal to emotion          | Using language or examples that trigger feelings such as fear, sympathy, anger or hope | Makes the reader feel emotionally involved and more likely to agree                           | “Imagine a child growing up without access to clean water.”          |
| Appeal to reason / logic   | Using logical arguments and clear cause-and-effect relationships                       | Makes the argument appear rational and convincing                                             | “If we reduce car use, we can lower emissions.”                      |
| Appeal to authority        | Referring to experts, scientists or respected institutions                             | Gives the argument credibility and makes it seem trustworthy                                  | “According to leading climate scientists…”                           |
| Statistics / data          | Using numbers, percentages or research findings                                        | Creates an impression of objectivity and factual accuracy                                     | “75% of students reported feeling stressed.”                         |
| Examples / anecdotes       | Providing specific cases or personal stories                                           | Makes abstract arguments more concrete and relatable                                          | “Take Sarah, a student who…”                                         |
| Personal experience        | Referring to the author's own experiences                                              | Creates authenticity and can establish an emotional connection                                | “When I was a student, I experienced…”                               |
| Rhetorical questions       | Questions that do not require an actual answer                                         | Encourages readers to think about the issue and can guide them toward the author's conclusion | “How can we ignore this problem?”                                    |
| Direct address             | Speaking directly to the reader using you                                              | Involves the reader personally and creates immediacy                                          | “You have the power to make a difference.”                           |
| Inclusive language         | Using we, us, our                                                                      | Creates a sense of community and shared responsibility                                        | “We must work together to solve this problem.”                       |
| Repetition                 | Repeating words, phrases or ideas                                                      | Emphasises key points and makes them memorable                                                | “We need change. We need action. We need it now.”                    |
| Anaphora                   | Repeating a word or phrase at the beginning of successive sentences/clauses            | Creates emphasis, rhythm and emotional intensity                                              | “We need courage. We need commitment. We need change.”               |
| Loaded / emotive language  | Words with strong positive or negative associations                                    | Influences the reader's emotional response to an issue                                        | “This cruel and unnecessary policy…”                                 |
| Hyperbole                  | Deliberate exaggeration                                                                | Makes an argument more striking or dramatic                                                   | “This decision will affect everyone on the planet.”                  |
| Contrast / juxtaposition   | Presenting opposing ideas or situations side by side                                   | Highlights differences and makes the author's position clearer                                | “Some live in luxury while others struggle to survive.”              |
| Metaphor                   | Describing something in terms of something else                                        | Makes complex ideas vivid and easier to understand                                            | “Social media is a double-edged sword.”                              |
| Analogy                    | Comparing one situation with another familiar one                                      | Helps readers understand or accept an argument                                                | “Managing a classroom is like conducting an orchestra.”              |
| Cause and effect           | Showing that one action or situation leads to another                                  | Makes the argument appear logical and inevitable                                              | “Excessive screen time can lead to poorer sleep.”                    |
| Counterargument + rebuttal | Mentioning an opposing view and then challenging it                                    | Makes the author seem balanced and strengthens their own position                             | “Some argue that…, however…”                                         |
| Concession                 | Acknowledging that an opposing argument has some validity                              | Makes the writer appear fair-minded and reasonable                                            | “While this approach may be expensive, it is ultimately worthwhile.” |
| Call to action             | Encouraging the reader to do something                                                 | Turns agreement into motivation or action                                                     | “It is time for us to take action.”                                  |
| Imperatives                | Using commands                                                                         | Creates urgency and encourages the reader to act                                              | “Consider the consequences. Take action.”                            |
| Word choice / diction      | Deliberately selecting particular words to shape the reader's perception               | Can make an issue appear positive, negative, urgent or harmless                               | “Freedom fighters” vs. “rebels”                                      |
| Tone                       | The writer's attitude conveyed through language                                        | Influences how the reader interprets the argument                                             | A confident, sarcastic or concerned tone                             |

# What to Write in an Analysis
Avoid simply writing:  
“The author uses statistics.”  
Instead, explain the purpose and effect:  
“The author uses statistics to provide factual evidence for her argument. By presenting numerical data, she makes her claims appear objective and credible, thereby encouraging the reader to accept her position.”
## A Useful Formula
TECHNIQUE → EVIDENCE → PURPOSE → EFFECT
### Example
The author uses rhetorical questions (“How can we ignore this problem?”) to challenge the reader and draw attention to the seriousness of the issue. This encourages the audience to reflect on their own responsibility and makes the author's position appear difficult to reject.

# A Useful Distinction
Technique ≠ effect  
For example:
- Technique: inclusive pronouns (we, us, our)
- Effect: creates a sense of unity and shared responsibility
- Purpose: makes the reader more receptive to the author's proposed solution

The manifesto "Woman and power" by Professor Mary Beard describes the many problems ans prejudices regarding woman in powerful positions.
Mary Beard wants to convey her message that woman in power should be a normal things and should be respected by others and not looked down upon as *"they don't deserve it"*. To convince the reader she uses a lot of of inclusive language ("we would all" (l.1), "If we close our eyes and try to conjure" (l.7)). To strengthen her point she gives some statistical evidence in lines 4 - 6, thereby making her claims appear more credible to urge the reader to accept her position. After that the author then presents a short anecdote about googling "cartoon professor". This story also provides some data (only 1 of 100 pictures includes a female professor) to build credibility. It is used to underline her personal experience "even if you are a woman professor: the cultural stereotype is so strong" (l.9-10) and to evoke the reader to think about these exact stereotypes. To highlight the problem, that we don't have any idea what a powerful woman should look like, she gives examples of powerful and famous woman and how they had to adapt to be accepted. The author try's to display these examples as bad examples by describing their clothing as “regulation trouser suits” (l.17) or "clothes horse" (l.20). These words have negative connotations, as “regulation” suggests that women have to follow certain rules, while “clothes horse” reduces them to their appearance.
